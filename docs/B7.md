# B7. Narration of the opponent's moves

B6 made the bot say why it moved. This increment makes it say what the other side's last move did, in the same words and from the same readings, so the chat reads as a running commentary on both sides of the board.

## What it is

- `src/narrate.rs`: a second function, `say_theirs(before, move)`, for a move that has already been played. `before` is the position it was played from. It reads the same loose-piece lists as `say`, from the other side's point of view: "you" is the side that just moved, "my" is the engine.
- `src/bin/uci.rs`: the engine now remembers the last move in the `position` command, and with `Narrate` on it prints both sentences on one line, `info string say: <their move> | <my move>`. With `Narrate` off the output is unchanged.
- `bot/homemade.py`: splits the line at the bar, and posts the sentences to the player chat in that order from a background thread, so the bot's next move never waits on a chat call.
- `tests/fixtures/games_sample.pgn`: the twelve most recent games in Brian's archive, moves only, 602 plies. They are the test set for this increment, because they are real games by a real player, and not positions picked for having a loose piece.
- `tools/narrate_check.py`: replays those games through the real `uci` binary and checks every claim in every sentence about a move against python-chess, which shares no code with the engine.

## What the sentence says

The first of these that applies, then any of the clauses that do:

1. A capture: "You took my loose knight on d5 with your pawn." ("loose" only if the engine's piece was loose before the capture), with "and promoted to a queen" when it promotes. En passant: "You took my pawn en passant."
2. Castling: "You castled kingside." or "You castled queenside."
3. A promotion: "You promoted to a queen on e8."
4. Any other move: "You moved your knight to f3."

Clauses, in this order:

- "It gives check."
- "That leaves your queen on d6 loose." for pieces of the mover that are loose after the move and were not before it. A piece that moved while loose is the same piece.
- "It attacks my rook on a1, which is loose." for the engine's pieces that are loose after the move and were not before it.
- "Your bishop on g5 is still loose." for the mover's pieces that were loose before the move and still are.

A line is at most 140 characters; the clauses at the end are the first to go when it is too long.

## Predictions

1. The words do not touch the move. With the move list given, at depth 3, the engine plays the same move with `Narrate` on and off at each of the 602 positions in the sample games where a move was played, 602 of 602.
2. The sentences about the played move are true. For all 602 moves, no claim in the sentence is false: the capture, the captured and capturing pieces, the squares, the castling side, the promotion, the check, and every loose piece named, each judged by `tools/narrate_check.py`.
3. They say what mattered. For each move after which python-chess finds a piece of the mover's that has newly become loose, the sentence names the most valuable such piece, in at least 95% of those moves.
4. Every sentence is at most 140 characters, and the line splits back into the two sentences that were joined.
5. In the first live game after the restart, the bot posts two sentences for each reply it makes (one for the move it was just played, then its own) and one for its first move if it is White, with no chat error in the log, a median chat call under 300 ms, and no move lost to the clock.
6. Brian reads his own moves' sentences in a live game and finds them accurate, flags any that mislead, and says whether two lines per move is too many.

Predictions 1 to 4 are checked by the gates and the check script. Predictions 5 and 6 need a live game.

## Not in this increment

Saying how much a move changed the evaluation, naming a better move, a quiet setting for spectators, and anything that decides what to say by asking a language model. Every sentence is still a template over the meters.

## Results

Predictions 1 to 4 were checked before the bot was restarted. All four held.

| # | Prediction | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Same move with `Narrate` on and off | Held. 602 of 602 positions, depth 3, move list given. |
| 2 | The sentences about the played move are true | Held. 602 moves, 922 claims, none false by `tools/narrate_check.py`. |
| 3 | The most valuable newly loose piece is named | Held, on a small count. 49 of 49 (100%); only 49 of the 602 moves left a piece of the mover's newly loose. |
| 4 | At most 140 characters, and the line splits back | Held. No sentence over 140; 590 lines carry both sentences and the 12 start positions carry one. |

Two limits on what that shows. First, `tools/narrate_check.py` writes the loose definition out a second time in Python, as `tools/loose_fixture.py` already did for the loose block's gate, so it checks that the sentences are built correctly from the definition, not that the definition is the right one. Second, the check was tried on seven controls: six deliberately wrong (a wrong piece, a wrong square, an invented check, an invented capture, an invented loose piece, and a check left unannounced) and one true claim. It caught all six wrong ones and passed the true one.

What the sentences look like, from the first game in the sample (Brian as White against Symbia):

    5b h6    You moved your pawn to h6. It attacks my bishop on g5, which is loose.
    10b Bxf1 You took my bishop on f1 with your bishop. That leaves your bishop on f1 loose.
    14b g4   You moved your pawn to g4. It attacks my queen on f3 and knight on h3, which are loose.
    16b gxf3 You took my loose queen on f3 with your pawn.

The sentence about a trade ("That leaves your bishop on f1 loose") is true and reads like a warning when it is only a recapture waiting to happen; the bot's own sentence next to it says what happens.

Predictions 5 and 6 need a live game and are open.
